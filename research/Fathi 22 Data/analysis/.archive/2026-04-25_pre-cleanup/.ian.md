# Study Mastery Guide — Fathi Fall 2022 Eye-Tracking Research

---

## Part One: What the Experiment Was

This study examined how people look at algorithm visualizations while learning. Participants watched animated explanations of two classic graph search algorithms — Breadth-First Search, which we call BFS, and Depth-First Search, which we call DFS — rendered in a visual style called Metal, which uses high contrast and stylized graphics. While they watched, a Tobii eye-tracker recorded their eye movements at sixty frames per second, capturing exactly where they looked, for how long, and in what sequence.

The screen was divided into two Regions of Interest, called AOIs. On the left was a pseudocode panel — the written algorithmic steps. On the right was a geospatial map — the visual graph the algorithm was traversing. The central question was: given both panels, how does a viewer allocate their attention between the code and the map? And how does that change depending on their programming experience?

Participants were divided into three experience groups. Group One had no programming experience at all. Group Two had brief knowledge of programming. Group Three had multiple years of formal programming coursework. The sample sizes were roughly balanced: about nineteen or twenty participants per group per algorithm condition.

## Part Two: The Eye-Tracking Metrics

Before we can understand the findings, you need to know exactly what each measurement means.

Time to First Fixation, abbreviated TTFF, is how long it takes from the start of the animation for the participant's eyes to first land on an area of interest. It is measured in milliseconds. A lower TTFF on the pseudocode means the viewer looked at the code sooner.

Total Fixation Duration, abbreviated TFD, is the total accumulated time, in seconds, that a participant spent fixating on an area of interest across the entire trial. This is the most direct measure of how much attention a viewer gave to a panel.

Fixation Count, abbreviated FC, is the raw number of individual fixation events on an area. A fixation is a period where the eyes are relatively stable, typically lasting a few hundred milliseconds, as opposed to a saccade, which is the rapid jump between fixation points.

Visit Count, abbreviated VC, is the number of separate entries into an area of interest. If you fixate the pseudocode panel, glance away to the map, then return — that counts as two visits. Visit count captures how often a viewer returned to an area, not just how long they stayed.

Fix Before is a derived count measuring how many fixations a participant made on other parts of the screen before their first fixation on the pseudocode. A high Fix Before score means the viewer spent a long time exploring elsewhere before ever consulting the code.

First Fixation Duration, abbreviated FFD, is the duration of the very first fixation on an area of interest. It captures the initial depth of engagement before any scanning or switching begins.

From these raw metrics, the analysis derived four composite metrics. The pseudocode-to-map ratio is Total Fixation Duration on the pseudocode divided by Total Fixation Duration on the map. A ratio above one means more time was spent on the code than the map. A ratio below one means the map dominated. This is the primary measure of attention allocation.

The scanner index is Visit Count on the pseudocode divided by Total Fixation Duration on the pseudocode. It measures how rapidly a viewer scans — many brief visits produce a high scanner index; a few deep dwell periods produce a low one.

Average fixation depth is Total Fixation Duration on the pseudocode divided by Fixation Count on the pseudocode. It is the mean duration of each individual fixation. High average fixation depth means the viewer tends to stay on each fixation point for a long time — a signature of deep reading or deliberate processing.

Switching rate is the total visit counts on both panels combined, divided by the total fixation duration on both panels combined. High switching rate means the viewer is rapidly alternating between code and map.

## Part Three: The Statistical Methods

This study used non-parametric statistics throughout. Non-parametric means we do not assume the data follows a normal distribution. Eye-tracking data is typically skewed and contains outliers, so non-parametric methods are more appropriate than standard t-tests or ANOVAs.

The Mann-Whitney U test compares two independent groups on a single continuous metric. Instead of comparing means, it compares the rank ordering of all values across both groups combined. If BFS participants tend to have higher ranks on TTFF than DFS participants, the test will detect that. The output is a U statistic and a p-value. We also report effect size as rank-biserial r, which ranges from negative one to positive one, where zero means no difference and one means complete separation between groups.

The Kruskal-Wallis test is the non-parametric equivalent of a one-way ANOVA. It tests whether the distribution of a metric differs across three or more groups. In this study it tests whether G1, G2, and G3 differ on a metric. A significant result tells you the groups differ somewhere, but not which specific pairs differ — for that, you run post-hoc pairwise Mann-Whitney tests with correction for multiple comparisons.

Spearman correlation is a rank-based correlation coefficient. Like Pearson's r, it ranges from negative one to positive one. Positive values mean both metrics tend to increase together. Negative values mean as one increases the other decreases. Unlike Pearson, Spearman does not assume linearity — it tests whether the relationship is monotonic, meaning consistently increasing or consistently decreasing, even if not in a straight line. This is reported throughout as lowercase r.

Fisher's z-transformation is the core method in the group-moderation analysis. The question it answers is: does the Spearman correlation between two metrics differ significantly between two groups? For example, is the correlation between scanner index and the pseudocode-to-map ratio significantly different in G1 versus G3? To test this, each group's Spearman r is transformed using the arctanh function, which is also called Fisher's z. The transformed values are subtracted and divided by a standard error that accounts for sample size. This produces a z-statistic whose p-value tells you whether the correlation itself — not just the levels — changed between groups. The study ran this test on every pair of metrics across every pair of groups, producing what is called a Fisher z-test battery. Of approximately one hundred and five pairs tested, twenty-seven showed significant differences at the p less than point zero five level, and twenty-five showed direction reversals from G1 to G3.

Partial Spearman correlation answers the question: what is the correlation between two metrics, controlling for a third variable? For example, what is the correlation between scanner index and ratio after removing the influence of group membership and algorithm assignment? This is done by rank-regressing both metrics on the control variables, taking the residuals, and computing Pearson correlation on those residuals. The resulting coefficient approximates a partial Spearman correlation and isolates the relationship that exists within groups and within algorithm conditions.

The attentional coupling hypothesis is a summary statistic. For each group, we compute the mean of the absolute values of all Spearman correlations across all metric pairs. A group with high mean absolute r has metrics that strongly co-vary with each other — the attention system is tightly organized. A group with low mean absolute r has independent metrics — knowing one tells you little about another. The hypothesis is that this coupling should increase with expertise as the viewer's attentional system becomes more structured and purposeful.

## Part Four: Finding One — Algorithm Determines When You First Look at the Code

This is the most statistically powerful finding in the dataset, with an effect size of rank-biserial r equal to point nine five four, which is extremely large.

BFS viewers look at the pseudocode almost immediately after the animation begins. Their median Time to First Fixation on the pseudocode is about nineteen milliseconds. DFS viewers accumulate a median of seventy-six fixations on other areas of the screen before ever looking at the pseudocode — compared to about fifty-five fixations for BFS viewers.

The reason comes from the visual structure of each algorithm. BFS begins by activating multiple nodes simultaneously in a broad wave. Several edges light up at once, which is visually confusing and hard to track. A viewer who doesn't already know what BFS looks like will almost immediately turn to the pseudocode to understand what is happening. DFS, by contrast, follows a single path recursively down one branch before backtracking. It produces a clear, followable trace that the viewer can watch without needing code support.

The implication is that the first few seconds of the animation establish the viewer's strategy for the entire trial. BFS forces early code contact; DFS allows avoidance. This is an algorithm-level design effect, not a viewer-level effect.

## Part Five: Finding Two — DFS Produces Deeper but Delayed Pseudocode Engagement

Once DFS viewers do look at the pseudocode — later than BFS viewers — they stay longer per fixation. The median average fixation depth is point six nine three seconds for DFS viewers versus point five four seven seconds for BFS viewers. This is a large effect, rank-biserial r of point four five zero.

The explanation is a trade-off between timing and quality. BFS viewers encounter the pseudocode early, but that early contact is shallow — they are glancing at the code to orient themselves and quickly returning to the map. DFS viewers arrive at the code later, but when they do, they have already watched enough of the algorithm to have context. The pseudocode becomes meaningful to them. Their fixations are longer because they are actually reading, not just checking.

This creates a design tension. If you want viewers to consult the code early, BFS-style visual complexity achieves that. If you want viewers to read the code deeply when they do engage, DFS-style visual clarity may actually be better. You probably cannot have both from a single design.

## Part Six: Finding Three — The Expertise Inverted-U

The pseudocode-to-map ratio peaks in Group Two, the intermediate experience group. Group One novices have a median ratio of about nine point six. Group Two intermediates peak at twelve point three. Group Three experts drop back down to six point one. This inverted-U shape holds across both BFS and DFS, and appears in Total Fixation Duration and average fixation depth as well.

The mechanism differs at each group. Novices have low ratios because they cannot parse pseudocode efficiently. They may look at it briefly, fail to extract meaning, and drift back to the map. Experts have low ratios because they recognize the algorithm from the visual graph pattern alone and only need to consult the code for brief verification. Intermediates are in the productive zone: they have enough background to use the pseudocode as a verification tool, checking each step of the code against their partial mental model of what the algorithm is doing. This produces maximum code engagement.

The design implication is that the pseudocode panel is essentially a Group Two affordance. It serves the audience with partial knowledge most effectively. Visualizations targeting novices would need additional scaffolding — annotated pseudocode, natural language explanations, or step-by-step breakdowns. Visualizations for experts can deprioritize the pseudocode panel without losing anything.

## Part Seven: Finding Four — The BFS-DFS Attention Gap Exists Only in Novices

The difference in pseudocode-to-map ratio between BFS and DFS conditions is statistically significant only in Group One, with a p-value of point zero four zero and an effect size of point four three. The effect disappears in Group Two, which has a p-value of point six zero, and completely vanishes in Group Three, which has a p-value of one point zero zero.

This is called an interaction effect between algorithm and group. It means the algorithm's influence on attention allocation depends on who is watching.

Novices don't have prior schemas for either BFS or DFS. When they watch BFS, the visual complexity genuinely pulls them toward the code; when they watch DFS, the visual clarity genuinely lets them stay on the map. For intermediates and experts, prior knowledge overrides the visual pull of the animation. They know what BFS and DFS look like. They allocate attention based on their existing mental model, not the immediate visual stimulus. The animation's design becomes irrelevant to where they look.

This means that any redesign aimed at equalizing attention between BFS and DFS conditions is only meaningful for introductory audiences. For experienced programmers, the visual design of the animation is effectively transparent.

## Part Eight: Finding Five — Gaze Style Is Stable; Attention Allocation Is Not

For the fifty-two participants who appeared in both BFS and DFS conditions, within-person correlations reveal a striking split. Some metrics are stable across conditions; others are not.

The pseudocode-to-map ratio has a within-person correlation of point one eight, which is not significant. This means knowing someone's ratio in BFS tells you almost nothing about their ratio in DFS. The ratio is driven by the algorithm, not the person.

Scanner index has a within-person correlation of point five nine, which is highly significant. Average fixation depth has a within-person correlation of point six three, also highly significant. These are personal traits. You can identify a viewer as a scanner or a deep reader from either condition, and that label transfers to the other condition.

This distinction has direct design and research implications. If you want to change how much pseudocode viewers read, target the animation design — this is an algorithm-level lever. If you want to change how viewers read, target the viewer — this is a curriculum-level intervention. These are fundamentally different interventions that affect fundamentally different variables.

## Part Nine: Finding Six — Group Two Shows Parallel Engagement

In Groups One and Three, fixation counts on the pseudocode and fixation counts on the map are negatively correlated. More fixations on the code means fewer on the map, and vice versa. This is a zero-sum attention allocation pattern.

In Group Two, the correlation is positive: r equals point three one seven, p equals point zero four nine. Intermediates who make many fixations on the pseudocode also make many fixations on the map. They are not trading one for the other — they are actively engaging with both panels simultaneously.

This is the signature of active dual-process learning. A viewer in this mode is genuinely using both representations in concert: reading a line of code, switching to the graph to verify the step, returning to the code for the next line. This is exactly what algorithm visualization is theoretically designed to produce. The finding tells us that it is working — but only for Group Two.

Group One viewers lack the schema to productively alternate between panels. Group Three viewers are efficient enough that they do not need to consult both — they know what to expect and extract what they need from one panel at a time.

## Part Ten: Finding Seven — Expertise Tightens the Attentional System

The mean absolute Spearman correlation across all metric pairs increases monotonically across groups. Group One has a mean absolute r of point two nine four. Group Two rises to point three three zero. Group Three reaches point three six nine.

The specific pairs that show the largest growth with expertise include: scanner index and ratio, which goes from essentially zero in Group One to negative point six seven in Group Three; scanner index and Total Fixation Duration on the map, which goes from near zero in Group One to positive point six six in Group Three; and ratio and switching rate, which strengthens from negative point two two to negative point six five.

The interpretation is that expertise does not just shift the level of any single metric. It reorganizes the entire covariance structure of the attention system. In novices, metrics are largely independent — knowing someone's scanner index tells you almost nothing about their ratio. In experts, metrics co-vary reliably. Each metric becomes a window into a single coherent attentional strategy.

This has a critical methodological implication. Eye-tracking studies of algorithm comprehension that pool participants across experience levels are mixing people whose attention is organized by fundamentally different mechanisms. Averaged results from such studies may reflect no one's actual attentional system — they are artifacts of the mixture.

## Part Eleven: The Visualization Principles

Each finding was visualized with two graphs — one traditional, one cutting-edge. The reason for the upgrade in each case is worth understanding.

A grouped boxplot is a standard publication format showing the median, quartiles, and outliers for each group. A raincloud plot adds a half-violin curve showing the full probability density, plus jittered raw data points. The upgrade matters because boxplots can hide bimodality — a distribution with two peaks looks identical to a unimodal one at the boxplot level.

A bar chart with error bars shows the group mean plus or minus one standard error. A ridge plot stacks kernel density curves vertically for each group or condition. The upgrade matters because a bar chart cannot tell you whether a shift in mean is a uniform translation of the whole distribution or a change in only one tail.

An interaction line plot connects group means across conditions with two lines. A slope chart draws a thin line for each individual participant connecting their value in one condition to their value in another. The upgrade matters because group means can show an interaction pattern that is driven by only a few participants — the slope chart shows immediately whether the effect is consistent or heterogeneous.

A two-by-two scatter plot places one metric on the x-axis and another on the y-axis, with one panel per condition. A dumbbell chart explicitly connects each participant's values across conditions as a horizontal line. The upgrade matters because a scatter cloud does not identify individuals — the dumbbell chart makes within-person patterns immediately visible.

A three-panel scatter with regression lines shows bivariate correlation within each group. A two-dimensional kernel density contour shows the joint probability density of both variables in a single smooth surface. The upgrade matters because a regression line can be misleading with small samples — the contour shows the actual shape and orientation of the data cloud.

Side-by-side correlation heatmaps show the full matrix of pairwise correlations for two groups using color. A correlation network graph represents the same information as a graph where nodes are metrics and edges connect pairs with correlations above a threshold. The upgrade matters because heatmaps require reading individual cells to understand structure — the network topology encodes the coupling hypothesis spatially and immediately shows which metrics are central versus peripheral.

## Part Twelve: The Limitations

Several limitations are important to understand. The Fisher z-test battery tested approximately one hundred and five metric pairs. At a significance threshold of point zero five, about five of those could be false positives by chance alone. Results at p less than point zero one are more reliable; results at p near point zero five should be treated as directional.

Each group had roughly thirty-eight to forty participants total across both algorithm conditions. This gives the Fisher z-tests limited power to detect small true differences in correlations. The study can reliably detect large moderation effects but would miss subtle ones.

The experience groups are not randomized. Programming experience is a pre-existing trait, not something experimenters assigned. This means the observed differences might reflect factors correlated with experience — motivation, general cognitive ability, familiarity with graphical interfaces — rather than programming knowledge itself.

The partial Spearman correlation method used is an approximation based on rank regression. It does not exactly equal the partial Spearman in all circumstances, particularly when the relationship between control variables and outcomes is nonlinear.

All results are conditioned on the Metal visualization style. They may not generalize to other visualization designs.

## Part Thirteen: Quick Reference Summary

Finding One — BFS viewers look at the pseudocode almost immediately. DFS viewers look at other things for much longer first. Effect size: rank-biserial r of point nine five four.

Finding Two — Once DFS viewers do engage the pseudocode, they stay longer per fixation. Average fixation depth is significantly higher for DFS. Effect size: r of point four five zero.

Finding Three — The pseudocode-to-map ratio peaks in Group Two. Novices and experts both have lower ratios. This is an inverted-U pattern across experience levels.

Finding Four — The BFS-versus-DFS attention gap is only significant in Group One. It disappears with experience. This is a Group-by-Algorithm interaction effect.

Finding Five — Gaze style metrics like scanner index and average fixation depth are stable individual traits across algorithms. The pseudocode-to-map ratio is not stable — it is algorithm-driven.

Finding Six — Group Two is the only group where fixation counts on code and fixation counts on the map are positively correlated. This is the signature of active dual-process learning.

Finding Seven — The mean absolute correlation across all metric pairs increases monotonically from Group One to Group Three. Expertise tightens the attentional system. Metrics that are independent in novices become strongly coupled in experts.
